USMLE Step 1 Pharmacology: High-Yield Topics You Need to Know

LearnForge • 2026 • USMLE Step 1

Pharmacology is a major component of USMLE Step 1 preparation because questions often require you to understand drug mechanisms, therapeutic effects, adverse effects and clinical applications.

Instead of trying to memorize every medication individually, organize drugs into classes and learn the relationship between mechanism, effect, toxicity and clinical use.

Study principle: For every important drug, ask four questions: What does it target? How does it work? What does it do clinically? What important adverse effect should you recognize?

1. Pharmacokinetics

Pharmacokinetics describes what the body does to a drug. Understanding the basic principles makes many pharmacology questions easier.

  • Absorption
  • Bioavailability
  • Distribution
  • Protein binding
  • Metabolism
  • Excretion
  • Half-life
  • Clearance
  • Volume of distribution
  • Steady-state concentration

Understand how changes in clearance, volume of distribution and dosing affect drug concentration over time.

2. Pharmacodynamics

Pharmacodynamics describes what a drug does to the body. Focus on receptors, dose-response relationships and drug interactions.

  • Agonists
  • Antagonists
  • Partial agonists
  • Competitive antagonists
  • Noncompetitive antagonists
  • Potency
  • Efficacy
  • Therapeutic index
  • Dose-response curves

Do not memorize receptor terminology without understanding how receptor activation changes the response curve.

3. Autonomic Pharmacology

Autonomic pharmacology is one of the most useful areas to organize by receptor.

Sympathetic System

  • Alpha-1 receptors
  • Alpha-2 receptors
  • Beta-1 receptors
  • Beta-2 receptors
  • Beta-3 receptors

Parasympathetic System

  • Muscarinic receptors
  • Nicotinic receptors
  • Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors
  • Antimuscarinic drugs

When studying autonomic drugs, connect the receptor with its normal physiological effect before memorizing individual medications.

High-yield method: Build a receptor table containing receptor location, signaling pathway, physiological effect, important agonists and important antagonists.

4. Cardiovascular Pharmacology

Cardiovascular drugs are easier to remember when grouped according to the physiological problem they treat.

  • ACE inhibitors
  • Angiotensin receptor blockers
  • Beta blockers
  • Calcium channel blockers
  • Diuretics
  • Nitrates
  • Alpha blockers
  • Antiarrhythmic drugs
  • Antiplatelet drugs
  • Anticoagulants

For each cardiovascular drug class, understand its effect on blood pressure, heart rate, cardiac contractility, vascular resistance or coagulation.

5. Diuretics

Diuretics are highly integrated with renal physiology. Understanding where each drug acts in the nephron is more useful than memorizing isolated facts.

  • Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors
  • Loop diuretics
  • Thiazide diuretics
  • Potassium-sparing diuretics
  • Aldosterone antagonists
  • Osmotic diuretics

Create a nephron diagram and place each drug class at its site of action. Then add its major electrolyte effects and important adverse effects.

Our USMLE Step 1 High-Yield Physiology guide can help you review the renal physiology behind these medications.

6. Antimicrobial Pharmacology

Antimicrobial drugs require integration of microbiology, pharmacology and clinical medicine.

  • Cell wall synthesis inhibitors
  • Protein synthesis inhibitors
  • Nucleic acid synthesis inhibitors
  • Folate pathway inhibitors
  • Membrane-disrupting drugs
  • Antimycobacterial drugs
  • Antifungal drugs
  • Antiviral drugs
  • Antiparasitic drugs

For each antimicrobial class, know the major target, mechanism of action, important organisms covered and characteristic adverse effects.

7. Beta-Lactam Antibiotics

Beta-lactam antibiotics are important because they provide a useful example of mechanism-based pharmacology.

  • Penicillins
  • Cephalosporins
  • Carbapenems
  • Monobactams
  • Beta-lactamase inhibitors

Focus on the bacterial target, resistance mechanisms, important clinical uses and characteristic adverse effects.

8. CNS Pharmacology

Central nervous system pharmacology contains many drug classes. Organize them according to the neurotransmitter system or clinical condition they affect.

  • Antidepressants
  • Antipsychotics
  • Anxiolytics
  • Antiepileptic drugs
  • Parkinson disease medications
  • Alzheimer disease medications
  • General anesthetics
  • Local anesthetics
  • Opioids

For CNS drugs, pay particular attention to mechanisms, major adverse effects, toxicity and important drug interactions.

9. Endocrine Pharmacology

Endocrine pharmacology becomes easier when connected to the normal hormone feedback systems.

  • Insulin preparations
  • Oral diabetes medications
  • Thyroid medications
  • Antithyroid drugs
  • Corticosteroids
  • Mineralocorticoid-related drugs
  • Sex hormones
  • Hormonal contraceptives
  • Bisphosphonates

When learning an endocrine drug, first understand the physiological pathway and then determine where the drug acts.

10. Gastrointestinal Pharmacology

GI pharmacology includes drugs that modify acid secretion, motility, nausea and intestinal function.

  • Proton pump inhibitors
  • H2 receptor blockers
  • Antacids
  • Antiemetics
  • Laxatives
  • Antidiarrheal drugs
  • Prokinetic drugs

Connect gastrointestinal drugs with the physiological processes described in our USMLE Step 1 Physiology guide.

11. Anti-Inflammatory Drugs

Anti-inflammatory pharmacology is closely connected with the inflammatory pathways studied in pathology.

  • NSAIDs
  • Acetaminophen
  • Glucocorticoids
  • DMARDs
  • Biologic therapies

Understand the target pathway and how inhibition changes inflammatory mediators.

For the underlying disease mechanisms, review our USMLE Step 1 High-Yield Pathology guide.

12. Chemotherapy and Cancer Pharmacology

Cancer pharmacology can seem difficult because many medications have complex mechanisms and toxicities.

  • DNA-damaging agents
  • Antimetabolites
  • Microtubule inhibitors
  • Topoisomerase inhibitors
  • Targeted therapies
  • Hormonal therapies
  • Immunotherapies

Focus on the relationship between the cellular target and the characteristic toxicity of each major drug class.

13. Toxicology and Antidotes

Recognizing common toxic exposures and their treatments can be valuable for Step 1 preparation.

  • Opioid toxicity
  • Acetaminophen toxicity
  • Organophosphate toxicity
  • Heavy metal poisoning
  • Carbon monoxide exposure
  • Methemoglobinemia
  • Common drug overdoses

Study toxicology using a simple structure: exposure, mechanism, clinical findings and treatment.

Clinical approach: When you see a toxicity question, identify the exposure first, recognize the physiological consequence and then choose the appropriate treatment or antidote.

14. Drug Adverse Effects

Adverse effects are often easier to remember when they are connected to the drug's mechanism.

Instead of creating a completely separate list, ask why a drug would produce a particular adverse effect based on its receptor, enzyme or physiological target.

  • Mechanism-related toxicity
  • Dose-dependent toxicity
  • Organ-specific toxicity
  • Drug-drug interactions
  • Contraindications
  • Pregnancy-related considerations

15. Drug Interactions

Drug interactions can occur through pharmacokinetic or pharmacodynamic mechanisms.

  • Enzyme induction
  • Enzyme inhibition
  • Changes in absorption
  • Changes in protein binding
  • Changes in renal elimination
  • Additive physiological effects
  • Antagonistic drug effects

Understanding the mechanism of an interaction helps you predict the clinical result rather than relying only on memorized pairs of medications.

How to Study Pharmacology Efficiently

A strong pharmacology study session should combine mechanisms, recall and clinical application.

  1. Start with the drug class.
  2. Identify the molecular or physiological target.
  3. Explain the mechanism of action.
  4. Connect the mechanism with the clinical effect.
  5. Learn the most important adverse effects.
  6. Practice questions.
  7. Review incorrect answers.
  8. Use spaced repetition for difficult drug classes.

Use Drug Tables

Drug tables can make pharmacology easier to review. A useful table can contain the drug class, mechanism, major indications, important adverse effects and contraindications.

Keep the table concise. The goal is to make review faster, not to create another massive textbook.

Connect Pharmacology With Anatomy and Physiology

Pharmacology becomes easier when it is connected to the underlying anatomy and physiology.

For example, cardiovascular drugs become easier to understand when you know how the heart, blood vessels and autonomic nervous system normally regulate blood pressure and cardiac output.

Review our USMLE Step 1 High-Yield Anatomy guide alongside physiology and pharmacology to build these connections.

Pharmacology and Your Step 1 Study Schedule

Pharmacology should be revisited throughout your Step 1 preparation rather than studied only once.

Use your practice-question performance to identify drug classes that need additional review.

If you are following a structured preparation plan, see our USMLE Step 1 6-Month Study Schedule .

Common Pharmacology Study Mistakes

  • Trying to memorize every medication individually.
  • Ignoring mechanisms of action.
  • Learning adverse effects without understanding why they occur.
  • Studying pharmacology separately from physiology.
  • Using too many resources simultaneously.
  • Not reviewing incorrect practice questions.
  • Failing to revisit difficult drug classes.

A Simple Pharmacology Review Cycle

A practical review cycle can be repeated for every major drug class.

  1. Learn the mechanism.
  2. Explain the mechanism from memory.
  3. Connect it to physiology.
  4. Identify the main clinical uses.
  5. Identify the major adverse effects.
  6. Answer practice questions.
  7. Review the class again after a spaced interval.

How to Prioritize High-Yield Pharmacology

When study time is limited, prioritize drug classes that connect with major organ systems and commonly tested clinical concepts.

  • Autonomic pharmacology
  • Cardiovascular drugs
  • Diuretics
  • Antimicrobials
  • CNS drugs
  • Endocrine drugs
  • Anti-inflammatory drugs
  • Toxicology and antidotes

After mastering these areas, use practice questions to identify additional topics that require attention.

Conclusion

High-yield pharmacology preparation for USMLE Step 1 is not simply about memorizing long lists of medications. The most useful approach is to organize drugs by class, understand their mechanisms, connect them to physiology and learn their clinically important adverse effects.

Use active recall, drug tables, practice questions and spaced review to build a pharmacology system that you can repeatedly revisit throughout your Step 1 preparation.
