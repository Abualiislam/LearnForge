USMLE Step 1 Physiology: High-Yield Topics You Need to Know

LearnForge • 2026 • USMLE Step 1

Physiology is one of the most important foundations for understanding USMLE Step 1. Many clinical questions require you to understand how a normal physiological system works before identifying what has gone wrong.

Instead of memorizing isolated facts, focus on mechanisms, relationships and predictable changes in response to disease or intervention.

Study principle: When studying physiology, always ask: What is changing, why is it changing, and what happens next?

1. Cardiovascular Physiology

Cardiovascular physiology is a major foundation for understanding hemodynamics and many clinical conditions.

  • Cardiac cycle
  • Cardiac output
  • Preload and afterload
  • Frank-Starling mechanism
  • Blood pressure regulation
  • Systemic vascular resistance
  • Pressure-volume loops
  • Autonomic regulation

Try to understand how changes in preload, afterload and contractility affect stroke volume and cardiac output rather than memorizing each relationship separately.

2. Respiratory Physiology

Respiratory physiology connects ventilation, perfusion and gas exchange with clinical problems involving the lungs.

  • Ventilation and perfusion
  • V/Q mismatch
  • Dead space
  • Alveolar ventilation
  • Oxygen transport
  • Carbon dioxide transport
  • Oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve
  • Acid-base effects on respiration

A useful strategy is to compare obstructive and restrictive patterns and connect pulmonary function changes to the underlying mechanism.

3. Renal Physiology

Renal physiology is highly integrated with electrolytes, acid-base balance and blood pressure regulation.

  • Glomerular filtration
  • Renal blood flow
  • Tubular reabsorption
  • Sodium handling
  • Potassium regulation
  • Water balance
  • Concentration and dilution of urine
  • Renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system
  • Acid-base regulation

Learn what happens in each nephron segment and connect transport processes with the hormones that regulate them.

4. Acid-Base Physiology

Acid-base questions become easier when you understand the relationship between carbon dioxide, bicarbonate and pH.

  • Metabolic acidosis
  • Metabolic alkalosis
  • Respiratory acidosis
  • Respiratory alkalosis
  • Compensation
  • Anion gap
  • Renal compensation

Rather than memorizing isolated values, practice identifying the primary disturbance and determining whether the expected compensation is present.

High-yield method: For every acid-base problem, identify the pH direction, determine the primary disturbance, evaluate compensation and then look for an additional process.

5. Endocrine Physiology

Endocrine physiology focuses heavily on feedback mechanisms and hormone relationships.

  • Hypothalamic-pituitary axes
  • Thyroid regulation
  • Adrenal hormones
  • Insulin and glucagon
  • Calcium regulation
  • Growth hormone
  • ADH
  • Aldosterone

Feedback loops are particularly important. When a target hormone decreases, ask how the upstream hormones should respond.

6. Gastrointestinal Physiology

GI physiology includes digestion, absorption, secretion and regulation of gastrointestinal motility.

  • Gastric acid secretion
  • Pancreatic secretion
  • Bile production and function
  • Intestinal absorption
  • GI hormones
  • Motility
  • Fluid and electrolyte absorption

Connect each GI hormone with its source, stimulus and major physiological effect.

7. Neurophysiology

Neurophysiology provides the foundation for understanding neurological symptoms and many pharmacological mechanisms.

  • Resting membrane potential
  • Action potentials
  • Synaptic transmission
  • Neurotransmitters
  • Reflexes
  • Autonomic nervous system
  • Sensory pathways
  • Motor control

Combine neurophysiology with anatomy. For example, understanding a neural pathway becomes more useful when you know where the pathway travels and what happens when it is damaged.

Our USMLE Step 1 High-Yield Anatomy guide can help you connect physiological mechanisms with anatomical structures.

8. Muscle Physiology

Muscle physiology is important for understanding movement, neuromuscular function and several clinical disorders.

  • Skeletal muscle contraction
  • Neuromuscular junction
  • Excitation-contraction coupling
  • Calcium regulation
  • Smooth muscle contraction
  • Cardiac muscle physiology

Focus on how calcium, actin, myosin and ATP interact during contraction.

9. Reproductive Physiology

Reproductive physiology requires understanding hormonal cycles and the relationships between the hypothalamus, pituitary and gonads.

  • Menstrual cycle
  • Ovulation
  • Estrogen and progesterone
  • Spermatogenesis
  • Testosterone regulation
  • Pregnancy physiology
  • Lactation

Use timelines and feedback diagrams to make reproductive hormone patterns easier to remember.

10. Hematology and Exercise Physiology

Physiology also explains how the body responds to changes in oxygen demand, blood volume and physical activity.

  • Oxygen delivery
  • Hemoglobin function
  • Exercise response
  • Blood volume regulation
  • Temperature regulation
  • Adaptation to altitude

How to Study Physiology Efficiently

A good physiology study session should move from understanding to recall and then to application.

  1. Learn the mechanism.
  2. Draw the pathway or feedback loop.
  3. Explain it without looking at your notes.
  4. Change one variable and predict the result.
  5. Answer practice questions.
  6. Review mistakes and weak concepts.

This method works particularly well when physiology is integrated with pathology and pharmacology.

Physiology and Your Step 1 Study Schedule

Physiology should not be studied as an isolated subject. Include it throughout your preparation and repeatedly revisit difficult mechanisms.

If you are building a structured six-month preparation plan, see our USMLE Step 1 6-Month Study Schedule .

Common Physiology Study Mistakes

  • Memorizing numbers without understanding mechanisms.
  • Ignoring graphs and physiological curves.
  • Studying physiology separately from pathology.
  • Not practicing prediction-based questions.
  • Using too many resources simultaneously.
  • Failing to review incorrect questions.

Conclusion

High-yield physiology preparation is about understanding how systems work and predicting how they respond to changes. Focus on cardiovascular, respiratory, renal, endocrine, neurological and acid-base physiology.

Use diagrams, active recall, practice questions and spaced review to turn physiology from memorization into a system of connected concepts.
