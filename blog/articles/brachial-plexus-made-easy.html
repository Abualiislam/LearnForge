Brachial Plexus Made Easy

LearnForge • USMLE Step 1 Study Guide

The brachial plexus is a network of spinal nerves that provides motor and sensory innervation to much of the upper limb. For USMLE Step 1, the easiest way to learn it is to understand its organization rather than memorize isolated nerves.

The Basic Organization

The classic sequence is:

Roots → Trunks → Divisions → Cords → Branches

The roots are formed by the anterior rami of spinal nerves C5 through T1. These roots combine to form three trunks, which divide into anterior and posterior divisions. The divisions then reorganize into three cords named according to their relationship to the axillary artery.

Roots

  • C5
  • C6
  • C7
  • C8
  • T1

A useful memory sequence is simply C5–T1. Clinical questions may describe an injury by root level rather than by the name of a peripheral nerve.

Trunks

  • Superior trunk: C5–C6
  • Middle trunk: C7
  • Inferior trunk: C8–T1

Divisions

Each trunk splits into an anterior division and a posterior division. The divisions are an important organizational step because they help separate the pathways supplying flexor and extensor compartments.

Cords

  • Lateral cord
  • Posterior cord
  • Medial cord

The cord names describe their relationship to the axillary artery. This is a high-yield anatomical relationship.

Major Terminal Branches

  • Musculocutaneous nerve
  • Axillary nerve
  • Radial nerve
  • Median nerve
  • Ulnar nerve
High-Yield Point: Think of the brachial plexus as a transformation: C5–T1 roots become trunks, trunks become divisions, divisions become cords, and cords give rise to major peripheral nerves.

How to Study It

Instead of repeatedly memorizing the entire diagram, study it in five passes: first the roots, then trunks, then divisions, then cords, and finally terminal branches. After each pass, redraw the structure from memory.

Conclusion

The brachial plexus becomes much easier when its architecture is understood. For exam preparation, connect each nerve to its roots, major motor functions, sensory territory and clinically important injury patterns.
